Build Specification · Version 1

FantasyGuru v1

A cross-league dashboard for Sleeper, and a Sunday-morning alarm that tells you whether any of your lineups are broken while you can still fix them.

Scope Sleeper only · redraft · Expo · Android first Target Week 5–6, mid-October 2026 Revised Aug 23, 2026 · rev 3
§1

Stack

This is a read-heavy, cache-dominated app with one hard scheduling requirement and no write path. That shape should drive every choice below — the interesting decisions are about caching, scheduling, and app-store lead times, not about the framework.

Android is the product; the other targets come free. Expo Router with React Native Web compiles the same source to Android, iOS, and a real website. v1 ships as an Android app, because that is where feedback will come from fastest and where the notification story has no caveats. iOS is built from what Android users report — not from guesses — and the web build costs almost nothing to keep as a front door.

1.1  The cron trap

Blocking constraint

Vercel's Hobby plan permits cron once per day, and fires it anywhere inside a ±59 minute window. Your entire product is a notification that must land between 10:00 and 11:00 ET on Sunday, ahead of the 1pm kickoffs. Hobby cannot do that, and a deploy with a more frequent cron expression fails at build time rather than degrading quietly.

Decided: an external scheduler. Upstash QStash calls a protected route on the backend service. Free tier, minute-level precision, no platform plan upgrade, and it stays correct if the backend later moves hosts. The alternative — GitHub Actions cron — is itself routinely 10–30 minutes late under load, which reintroduces the exact problem being solved.

The scheduled route must be idempotent and gated behind a shared secret. External schedulers retry on timeout, and a retry must never mean a second notification. The unique constraint on alarms_sent in §2.3 is what enforces this.

1.2  Split the backend out from the client

Once the client is Expo rather than Next.js, there is no framework left holding the API, and that is a gift rather than a problem. Put the sync loops, the rule engine, and the alarm dispatcher in a standalone service that all three clients call.

The rule engine in particular should be pure functions over plain JSON — no framework, no database access, no HTTP. It takes rosters, players, and league settings; it returns a list of problems. That makes it trivially testable against fixtures, and it is the one piece of this codebase that genuinely must not have bugs.

Resist Expo's own API Routes for this. They are real and improving, but your backend needs scheduled jobs, a concurrency limiter, and a retry queue — that wants a boring always-on server, not the newest thing in the ecosystem.

1.3  The store gates — do this arithmetic before anything else

With iOS deferred and the September 9 deadline released, one gate remains and it is now a scheduling item rather than a crisis.

  • Google Play closed testing — personal accounts created after 13 Nov 2023 must keep at least 12 testers opted in continuously for 14 days, then apply for production access, which Google reviews in up to 7 more days. Roughly 21 days end to end.
  • Apple Developer enrollment — Deferred off the critical path entirely. Worth starting the $99 enrollment anyway, because it is free waiting that runs in the background and non-US individuals see 2 days to 2 weeks.

Start the closed test the day you have a build that launches, however unfinished. Twelve testers for fourteen days is exactly what league-mates are for — name them this week, because the clock does not start until they are opted in.

1.4  Week 1 was the worst week to launch this. Mid-October is the best.

The deadline slipping is an upgrade, not a compromise

There are no bye weeks in the 2026 NFL season until Week 5. Byes run Week 5 through Week 14, peaking at six teams in Week 11, with none at all in Week 12. Rule 2 — bye-week player in a starting slot — is one of your three critical alarms, and for the first four weeks of the season it is physically incapable of firing. Injury designations are also thin early, before the attrition accumulates.

A Week 1 launch would have shipped the alarm into the four weeks it has least to say. Launching around Week 5–6, mid-October, puts first contact in the window where the product is loudest: four teams on bye in Week 6, four in Week 7, four in Week 8, six in Week 11. Every one of those is a week where a real user gets a real save.

This also buys back the scope that was cut under deadline pressure. Rules 4–7 move back in. The live scoreboard becomes viable. Rule 7 gets tuned against several weeks of real league data instead of guesswork — which matters, because it is the rule most likely to misfire.

The cost is that you launch into a season already in progress, so the first-run experience needs to handle a user arriving in Week 6 with five weeks of history behind them. That is a smaller problem than shipping an alarm with nothing to report.

§2

How the app works

2.1  Onboarding is the whole advantage — protect it

Sleeper's API needs no token, which means your first-run experience can be something no competitor with an ESPN or Yahoo integration can match:

  1. One input on the landing page: your Sleeper username.
  2. GET /v1/user/{username} → user_id.
  3. GET /v1/user/{user_id}/leagues/nfl/2026 → every league.
  4. Dashboard renders. Under ten seconds, no password, no OAuth, no email.
  5. Only then — after they have seen their own leagues on screen — do you ask for anything.

That ordering is not a nicety, it is the product's single largest structural advantage. Every signup field you add before step 4 spends it.

Data model gotcha

Sleeper usernames are mutable. Persist the user_id as the identity and treat the username purely as a lookup key, or returning users will silently fork into new accounts.

2.2  Three sync loops

Loop A · Daily 03:00 ET

Player universe

GET /v1/players/nfl — roughly 5MB and 11k players. Fetch it once globally, never per user or per request, and write it into a players table.

This is the single most consequential caching decision in the app. Also refresh /v1/state/nfl for the current week.

Loop B · On demand, cached

League state

Keyed by league_id, not by user — two members of the same league share one cache entry, which materially changes your rate-limit math.

  • /league/{id} — settings, scoring, roster slots
  • /league/{id}/rosters
  • /league/{id}/users
  • /league/{id}/matchups/{week}

Loop C · Scheduled

Alarm evaluation

Sun 09:00 and 11:30 ET, plus Thu 18:00 and Mon 18:00 ET for the standalone games.

For each registered user: sync leagues, run the rule engine, diff against alarms already sent, dispatch only what is new.

Cache TTL by time of week

A fixed TTL is wrong for this app. Nothing moves Tuesday through Friday; everything moves in one four-hour window.

WindowTTLReasoning
Tue – Fri6 hoursStatic. One refresh after Wednesday waivers processes covers the whole stretch.
Saturday1 hourLineups begin moving; injury designations firm up.
Sun 06:00 – 13:00 ET5 minutesPeak. Everyone is setting lineups at once and stale data is actively harmful.
Sun 13:00 – 24:00 ET60 secondsGames live. Scores only — rosters are locked.
Monday15 minutesMNF, plus the aftermath view.

2.3  Rate-limit math

Sleeper's published guidance is to stay under 1,000 calls per minute or risk an IP block. One league sync is four calls. At 1,000 users averaging three leagues you have ~3,000 memberships, but overlap means perhaps 2,200 distinct leagues — roughly 8,800 calls per full sweep. Spread across a ten-minute window with a concurrency limiter at ~10 you sit near 880/min. That is workable and it is not comfortable.

Build the concurrency limiter and the per-league cache on day one. Retrofitting either one under real load, on a Sunday morning, with an IP block in progress, is the failure mode that ends the project.

Core schema — the seven tables v1 actually needs 
users            id, sleeper_user_id, username, email, tz,
                 push_subscription, created_at

leagues          sleeper_league_id, name, season, scoring_settings,
                 roster_positions, total_rosters, synced_at

memberships      user_id, league_id, roster_id           -- join table

rosters          league_id, roster_id, owner_user_id,
                 players[], starters[], synced_at

players          sleeper_player_id, full_name, team, position,
                 status, injury_status, practice_participation,
                 depth_chart_order, updated_at

team_bye_weeks   team (32 rows), bye_week                -- hand-maintained

alarms_sent      user_id, league_id, rule, player_id, week, sent_at
                 -- UNIQUE(user_id, league_id, rule, player_id, week)

The unique constraint on alarms_sent is what makes the notification path idempotent. It is one line and it is the difference between a useful app and one people mute in week two.

§3

The Lineup Alarm

Every rule returns a fact, never an opinion. That constraint is the feature — it means the app is never embarrassingly wrong in front of someone using it for the first time, which is a promise no projection-based tool can make.

#RuleSeverityDetection
1Empty starting slotCriticalstarters[i] == "0" or null
2Bye-week player in a starting slotCriticalplayer.team ∈ byeWeeks[week]
3OUT / IR / SUS / PUP startingCriticalinjury_status ∈ {Out, IR, Sus, PUP}
4DOUBTFUL startingWarninginjury_status == "Doubtful"
5QUESTIONABLE starting, did not practiceInfostatus == "Questionable" &&
practice_participation == "DNP"
6Roster below league minimumWarningcount(players) < minimum
7Cross-league inconsistency — started here, benched thereInfopid ∈ A.starters &&
pid ∈ (B.players − B.starters)

Rule 7 is the one nobody else can compute, because nobody else holds all of a person's leagues at once. It is also the one that will destroy the product if it is naive.

Rule 7 suppression clause

Fire only when the benched instance is not forced by roster construction — that is, the player would be legal in an open or flex slot in league B, and the player currently occupying that slot has a worse injury status or the slot is empty. Without this clause the rule fires on nearly every shared player every week, means nothing, and teaches people to ignore your notifications.

Missing data — must be supplied by you

The Sleeper player object carries status, injury_status, and practice_participation, but no bye week. You need your own 32-row team_bye_weeks table, hand-entered from the published schedule. It is the cheapest artifact in this entire spec and by far the most humiliating one to get wrong — a false bye-week alarm in Week 5 is worse than no alarm at all. Enter it, then verify it a second time from a different source.

3.1  Notification discipline

This section, more than the rule table, determines whether the app survives to Week 4.

  • Push only for Critical, and only in the Sunday-morning and Thursday/Monday-evening windows.
  • One notification per user per window, aggregated — "3 problems across 2 leagues" — never one per problem.
  • Never send the same alarm twice. That is what alarms_sent is for.
  • Never between midnight and 07:00 local. Store the user's timezone at signup.
  • Hard ceiling of four pushes per user per week. If you would exceed it, drop the lowest severity rather than raising the cap.
§4

Look, feel, and the weekly hook

4.1  The first second answers one question

Not a nav bar, not a logo, not a stat grid. The top of the dashboard is a single unambiguous verdict, and everything else on the page is supporting material.

Sunday 10:14 AM · Week 3

You're set.

All 4 lineups are legal and healthy. Next check at 11:30.

Sunday 10:14 AM · Week 3

3 things need you.

Across 2 of your 4 leagues · 2h 46m until kickoff

Puka Nacua is on bye — starting at WR2 DYNASTY DEGENERATES FIX →
Empty FLEX slot THE LEAGUE OF ORDINARY GENTLEMEN FIX →
You started Bijan here, benched him in Ordinary Gentlemen DYNASTY DEGENERATES · CROSS-LEAGUE VIEW →

Both states of the verdict. The calm state is not a placeholder — it is the state most users will see most weeks, and it should feel like a closed loop rather than an empty screen.

Design the calm state first

Most weeks, for most users, nothing is wrong. Most apps punish that with a wall of data. "You're set." — large, green, no detail — is the reward for opening the app, and it is what makes opening it a habit rather than a chore.

4.2  Deep links are load-bearing

The API is read-only. You cannot fix anything. So every single alarm must terminate in a one-tap jump to the exact place in Sleeper where the fix happens. If the user has to go find the league themselves, you have added a step to their Sunday instead of removing one, and that is the entire difference between useful and irritating.

Unverified — check this in week one

Sleeper does not publish a deep-link scheme, and I could not confirm that a documented sleeper:// URI or universal link exists. Test this on day one, not on day fourteen. Open a league in the Sleeper mobile app and inspect the share sheet; check whether sleeper.com/leagues/{id} triggers a universal link into the installed app.

Going native improves the odds here: Linking.openURL() from one installed app to another is a first-class path, where a browser handing off to an app is not. But it does not create a scheme that does not exist. If none does, the fallback is the league URL in a browser — noticeably worse, and it reshapes the alarm itself: fewer and higher-value alerts, because each one now costs the user more effort to act on.

4.3  Design for four moments, not for daily active users

Fantasy football is not a daily-use product. Building for daily engagement produces a worse app and a frustrated founder.

4.4  The sleeper hit: the cross-league scoreboard

Every matchup you have, in every league, on one screen, updating through Sunday afternoon. It is cheap — the matchups endpoint returns points already computed in each league's own scoring — and it is the screen people will leave open on a second monitor for four hours. It is also, by some distance, the most likely thing here to get screenshotted into a group chat. If you find a spare weekend before Week 1, build this before anything else optional.

4.5  Visual direction

  • Dark-first. This gets used on a couch, and every app it sits beside is dark. Light mode is polite, not primary.
  • Dense, not airy. Fantasy players are comfortable with a lot of numbers. Over-whitespacing this into a marketing page makes it feel like a toy. It is a tool.
  • Colour means status and nothing else. Red is broken, amber is risk, green is fine, grey is everything else. The moment you use red as a brand accent you have destroyed the vocabulary the alarm depends on — so the brand accent must live outside the traffic-light range entirely.
  • Team colour and a position chip carry player identity, not headshots. Headshots are a licensing question you do not want in v1.
  • One big number per row. Points. Everything else is secondary text at secondary weight.
  • Speed is part of the aesthetic. Server-render everything; the dashboard should be painted before a spinner would have had cause to appear.

4.6  The mechanic that actually creates loyalty

It is not streaks and it is not badges. It is this: the app tells you something true that you did not know, at a moment when you can still act on it.

Rule 7 is the purest example — a small, specific, faintly embarrassing revelation that makes it obvious the app is paying attention on your behalf. One of those per season buys a year of loyalty. Chase more moments of that kind; do not chase daily opens.

§5

Production workflow

5.1  Repo, environments, CI

  • Single Next.js repo — FantasyGuru is already initialised and empty.
  • main deploys to production. Feature branches get a Vercel preview plus a matching Neon database branch.
  • TypeScript strict, ESLint, Prettier, enforced through lint-staged and husky so CI never has to argue about formatting.
  • GitHub Actions on every PR: typecheck, lint, test, build.
  • Drizzle Kit migrations, committed and applied on deploy. Additive only between September and January — no destructive migrations during the season.

The only tests that matter in v1

The alarm rule engine, tested against fixture JSON captured from real Sleeper responses — every rule gets one fixture where it must fire and one where it must not. Not the UI. This is the only part of the app where a bug is publicly humiliating, and fixtures are also how you keep working through the offseason when live data is meaningless. Capture a set of real league payloads into /fixtures in week one, before you need them.

5.2  Observability

  • Sentry for exceptions.
  • A sync_log table — league, timestamp, duration, outcome. When Sleeper changes something you find out in minutes rather than on a Sunday.
  • A synthetic canary: one known public Sleeper league, synced every 15 minutes, asserted on shape. If a field name changes upstream this fires before a single user notices.
  • Alert on: sync failure rate above 5%; the alarm job not completed by 09:15 ET Sunday; Sleeper p95 latency above 2s.

5.3  Notifications on Android

Android-first removes the hardest delivery problem in the original spec. There is no add-to-home-screen requirement, no Safari-tab caveat, no install-rate contingency. expo-notifications delivers through FCM straight to the lock screen, which is exactly where "your lineup is broken and kickoff is in two hours" belongs.

  • Android 13 and up require a runtime permission (POST_NOTIFICATIONS). It is a real prompt the user can decline, and once declined it is painful to recover. So earn it before you ask — show your own pre-prompt only after the user has seen their leagues on screen, explaining that they will hear from you rarely and only when something is actually broken. Never request it on first launch.
  • Use notification channels deliberately. One channel for critical lineup alarms, a separate one for everything else, so a user can mute the noise without muting the alarm. Android surfaces these in system settings whether you design them or not.
  • Email as the floor. Collect the address in the same interaction where you offer notifications. It survives a declined permission, a reinstall, and a new phone.

When iOS arrives later it inherits this design unchanged — the phased-channel complexity that dominated rev 2 simply disappears.

5.4  The release calendar is not normal

Never deploy Saturday or Sunday

Eighty percent of the value this app delivers is concentrated in one four-hour window per week. A bad deploy at 10am Sunday is not a rollback — the moment passes and does not return for seven days. Deploy Monday night through Thursday, treat Thursday as the last safe day, and feature-flag anything that touches the alarm path.

Pre-season checklist — before Sept 9

  1. Bye-week table entered and verified twice, from two different sources.
  2. Rate limiter proven against a simulated 500-user sweep.
  3. Alarm job dry-run every preseason Sunday, delivering to you alone.
  4. Rule 7 tuned against your own real leagues until it stops crying wolf.
  5. Home-screen install flow tested on a physical iPhone, not a simulator.
  6. A notifications kill switch that works without a deploy.

§7

Build order

Ordered so that the app is shippable at the end of every single day. If you run out of time on day 12, what exists on day 11 still works and is still worth using. Days 1–11 are backend and logic and are identical whether the client is web or native — the platform choice only bites from day 12.

If you fall behind

Cut in this order: rule 7, then rules 4–6, then web push, then the live scoreboard. Do not cut the per-league cache, the rate limiter, the bye-week verification, or alarms_sent. Those four are what stand between a soft launch and a public failure on the one Sunday people are watching.
